Results

CPMP mental health study

Statistical analyses

Age and home medicine count were summarised as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests used a tie-adjusted normal approximation with continuity correction to compare their distributions without requiring normally distributed measurements. Categorical variables were summarised as counts and percentages. Pearson chi-square tests compared proportions between periods, with Yates' continuity correction for 2 × 2 comparisons. Two-sided Fisher exact tests were used for the additional ward-timing comparisons because expected counts were below five. Diagnosis was compared using one global Pearson chi-square test across four mutually exclusive categories. Yates' correction does not apply to this 2 × 4 table. All reported Pearson chi-square comparisons had expected counts of at least five.

The primary outcome was at least one admission medication error, including duplication errors. The unadjusted chi-square test assessed its association with implementation period. Binomial logistic regression with a logit link estimated this association after adjustment for home medicine count, to account for differences in medication burden. An expanded sensitivity model added age, sex and diagnosis to assess the association after further case-mix adjustment. Diagnosis comprised psychotic illness (including drug-induced psychosis), bipolar affective disorder (BPAD), major depressive disorder (MDD) and Other. Covariates were specified independently of baseline p values. Home medicine count and age were entered as linear continuous terms on the log-odds scale. Odds ratios (ORs), Wald 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided Wald p values were calculated.

Adjusted risks were obtained by marginal standardisation, averaging predicted probabilities under each period across the same pooled 133-admission covariate distribution. These estimates express the model results as probabilities, risk ratios and absolute risk differences. Model-based delta-method 95% CIs used the full coefficient covariance matrix, treating the observed covariate distribution as fixed. Intervals used the logit scale for risks, the log scale for risk ratios and the direct scale for risk differences.

Secondary outcomes were total error count, any medication omission, omission count, and completion of best possible medication history (BPMH) and medication reconciliation within 24 hours. Negative binomial regression with a log link modelled error and omission counts to allow variability greater than the mean. Both count models adjusted for home medicine count, entered as a continuous linear term on the log-count scale. All admissions contributed, including those with zero errors. With one admission as the unit of analysis and no exposure offset, exponentiated coefficients represent expected count ratios per admission. Binomial logistic regression for any omission used the same adjustment and risk-standardisation methods as the primary model. Secondary analyses were added after reviewing the primary results and are exploratory. Their p values and 95% CIs have no adjustment for multiple comparisons.

Natural cubic splines of home medicine count (3 degrees of freedom) assessed sensitivity of the secondary regression estimates to the linear covariate assumption. Timing outcomes were reconstructed from original event timestamps, using actual reconciliation completion in both periods. ED-arrival and ward-admission starting points were analysed separately. Timing comparisons used available recorded outcomes, with explicit missing counts and bounds obtained by assigning unknown outcomes to either timely or delayed completion. No outcome was imputed in the main timing comparisons.

An additional sensitivity analysis excluded duplication errors from the outcome definition and repeated the unadjusted comparison and both logistic models. This assessed whether the period association depended on counting duplication errors. All admissions were retained. The models assumed independent admissions. Estimates describe associations in this observational before-and-after comparison and remain susceptible to residual confounding and changes over time.

Study population and baseline characteristics

The analysis included 133 admissions, comprising 69 before and 64 after CPMP implementation. Baseline, primary-outcome and error-count model variables were complete. Timing analyses used the outcome-specific denominators reported below. Baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Median age was 37 (IQR 31 to 49) years before implementation and 44.5 (IQR 33.5 to 53.5) years after implementation. Female patients accounted for 47.8% and 37.5% of admissions, respectively. The median number of home medicines was higher after implementation, at 5 (IQR 3 to 7), compared with 3 (IQR 1 to 5) before implementation (p < 0.001). Psychotic illness was the most common diagnostic category in both periods, accounting for 56.5% and 57.8% of admissions, respectively.

Primary outcome

At least one admission medication error, including duplication errors, occurred in 34 of 69 pre-implementation admissions (49.3%) and 22 of 64 post-implementation admissions (34.4%) (Table 2). The unadjusted comparison yielded a Yates-corrected chi-square statistic of 2.444 (df = 1, p = 0.118).

Primary adjusted analysis

After adjustment for the number of home medicines, the post-implementation period was associated with lower odds of an admission medication error (adjusted OR 0.310, 95% CI 0.137 to 0.702, p = 0.005). Each additional home medicine was associated with higher odds of an error (adjusted OR 1.245, 95% CI 1.107 to 1.401, p < 0.001) (Table 3).

Secondary model checks

Conditional Poisson models showed overdispersion, with Pearson dispersion statistics of 1.81 for total errors and 1.79 for omissions. Both negative binomial models converged, with corresponding Pearson statistics of 0.92 and 0.88. All admissions were retained, including the admission with ten errors.

Allowing a nonlinear association with home medicine count using a natural cubic spline (3 degrees of freedom) gave similar period count ratios for total errors (0.341, 95% CI 0.201 to 0.578) and omissions (0.294, 95% CI 0.148 to 0.582). For any omission, the spline-adjusted OR was 0.153 (95% CI 0.057 to 0.412), with standardised risks of 44.2% before implementation and 15.2% after implementation. The direction of each association was unchanged.

Absolute standardised omission counts were sensitive to extrapolation at high home medicine counts and are therefore not presented. Count-model results are summarised using adjusted count ratios alongside observed means. The standardised probabilities of any omission are reported separately.

Secondary outcomes: completion by 24 hours after ED arrival

The hospital-arrival comparison included 120 admissions with a recorded ED arrival time (69 pre-implementation and 51 post-implementation). Thirteen post-implementation admissions, comprising 11 interhospital transfers and two direct admissions, had no comparable ED arrival timestamp and were outside this comparison. Both periods used actual BPMH and medication reconciliation completion timestamps.

BPMH was completed by 24 hours after ED arrival in 33 of 69 pre-implementation admissions (47.8%) and 43 of 51 post-implementation admissions (84.3%). The Yates-corrected Pearson chi-square statistic was 15.278 (df = 1, p < 0.001). Among admissions with a recorded reconciliation completion time, reconciliation was completed by 24 hours in 32 of 65 (49.2%) and 42 of 50 (84.0%), respectively (Yates-corrected chi-square 13.415, df = 1, p < 0.001) (Table 18).
